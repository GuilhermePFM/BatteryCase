# Battery dispatch under stochastic prices

Formulation of the battery dispatch problem when the two price series are not
known in advance: a multistage stochastic mixed-integer linear program whose
deterministic counterpart is [`model.md`](model.md).

Throughout, **M§4.2** means §4.2 of that document. This file states the model
only. Nothing here is implemented, and no solution method is proposed; §11 says
what is deferred and why.

---

## 1. What changes, and what does not

M§1 takes both price series as exogenous **and known**: the whole horizon is
given before any decision is made, so the schedule is a single sequence chosen
with perfect foresight. The battery remains a price taker here, and the series
remain exogenous, but they are no longer known. A decision taken in period $t$
may depend only on what has been observed up to $t$.

Three things follow, and they are the whole content of this document:

- The decision is no longer a schedule but a **policy**: a rule mapping
  observed history to an action. On a finite scenario tree a policy is
  represented by one copy of the decision variables per node.
- The objective is no longer a number but a **functional of a distribution**.
  Expected profit is the default (§4); a mean-risk version is in §7.
- The cycle budget and the degradation of M§4.6 and M§4.7 are **path
  dependent**, so the cap admits more than one reading (§5.8).

What does not change: the physics. Every constraint of M§4.1 through M§4.7
holds in each node exactly as written, because none of them involves a price.
The model stays a MILP, because within a node the prices are data and revenue
is linear in the decisions (§6).

## 2. Uncertainty and information

### 2.1 Probability space and filtration

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ carry the joint price process
$\bigl(p_1(t), p_2(t)\bigr)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$, and let

$$
\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal{F}_K = \mathcal{F}
$$

be the filtration generated by observing prices as they settle, with
$\mathcal{F}_0$ trivial. A decision taken at stage $k$ must be
$\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable. That requirement is **non-anticipativity**, and it
is the only structural addition to M§4.

### 2.2 The scenario tree

The filtration is represented by a finite tree. Let

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $k = 0, \dots, K-1$ | stage, a decision epoch |
| $\mathcal{N}_k$ | the nodes at stage $k$; $\mathcal{N} = \bigcup_k \mathcal{N}_k$ |
| $r$ | the root, the single node of $\mathcal{N}_0$ |
| $a(n)$ | the parent of node $n \neq r$ |
| $\mathcal{C}(n)$ | the children of $n$ |
| $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{N}_{K-1}$ | the leaves, one per scenario |
| $\text{path}(n)$ | the nodes from $r$ to $n$ inclusive |
| $\mathrm{anc}^{\ell}(n)$ | the ancestor $\ell$ stages above $n$, with $\mathrm{anc}^0(n) = n$ |
| $\pi(n) > 0$ | the probability of reaching $n$ |

with $\pi(r) = 1$, $\pi(n) = \sum_{n' \in \mathcal{C}(n)} \pi(n')$, and hence
$\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}_k} \pi(n) = 1$ for every stage $k$. A node is an
information state: two scenarios sharing a node are indistinguishable up to
that stage.

### 2.3 Stages, periods, and the price data of a node

Decisions are taken more often than prices are re-forecast, so a stage covers
several settlement periods. Let $W$ be the number of half-hourly periods in one
stage, a whole number of hours so that no hourly interval of M§4.5 straddles a
stage boundary, and index periods within a node by $\tau = 1, \dots, W$. The
horizon is $T = K \, W$.

```
stage 0          stage 1              stage 2
node r           nodes               nodes
[τ=1..W]  ─┬──→  [τ=1..W]  ─┬──→     [τ=1..W]      scenario ω₁
           │                └──→     [τ=1..W]      scenario ω₂
           └──→  [τ=1..W]  ─┬──→     [τ=1..W]      scenario ω₃
                            └──→     [τ=1..W]      scenario ω₄

       S, n_cyc cross each arrow;  prices are revealed at each node
```

Each node $n$ carries the realised price vectors
$p_1(n, \tau)$ and $p_2(n, \tau)$ for $\tau = 1, \dots, W$. These are **data of
the node**, not variables, which is what keeps the program linear. Because a
node holds the joint realisation of both markets, any dependence between
$p_1$ and $p_2$, contemporaneous or lagged, is represented exactly by the tree
and needs no separate correlation structure in the constraints.

With $W = 48$ a stage is a day, and the tree branches once a day. $W = T$ and
$K = 1$ gives a two-stage problem; $W = 1$ gives a stage per settlement period.

### 2.4 When each market is committed

M§4.5 holds Market 2's decisions flat across each hour because capacity sold to
an hourly product is committed for the hour. Under uncertainty the same question
arises one level up: **is that commitment made before or after the node's prices
are observed?** Introduce, for each market, an information lag in stages:

| Symbol | Default | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ell_1$ | 0 | stages between committing a Market 1 volume and delivering it |
| $\ell_2$ | 0 or 1 | the same for Market 2 |

$\ell_m = 0$ means the volume for the periods of node $n$ is chosen at $n$,
knowing $p_m(n, \cdot)$. $\ell_m = 1$ means it is chosen at $a(n)$, before the
node is known, and must therefore be the same across all children of $a(n)$.

Setting $\ell_1 = \ell_2 = 0$ keeps the information structure of the brief, in
which both markets are read at the same resolution of knowledge and the only
uncertainty is about later stages. Setting $\ell_2 = 1$ with a daily stage models
Market 2 as a day-ahead product with gate closure the previous day, which is the
reading under which the two markets differ in kind rather than in settlement
frequency alone. The constraint that enforces either reading is §5.6. The choice is a
modelling decision this document adds; the brief does not state gate closures.

## 3. Notation

Sets and parameters of M§2 carry over unchanged. Added:

| Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $W$ | periods | settlement periods per stage |
| $K$ | – | number of stages |
| $\pi(n)$ | – | probability of node $n$ |
| $\ell_m$ | stages | commitment lag of market $m$ (§2.4) |
| $\alpha$ | – | CVaR tail probability (§7) |
| $\beta$ | – | risk weight, $\beta \in [0, 1]$ (§7) |
| $\varepsilon$ | – | permitted probability of exceeding the cycle budget (§5.8) |
| $\overline{Z}$ | cycles | big-$M$ for the chance-constrained cap (§5.8) |

Every decision variable of M§2 gains a node index and a within-node period
index. For $n \in \mathcal{N}$ and $\tau = 1, \dots, W$:

| Symbol | Domain | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $c_1(n, \tau),\ c_2(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MW | charging power bought from each market |
| $d_1(n, \tau),\ d_2(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MW | discharging power drawn from storage for each market |
| $S(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MWh | energy stored at the end of the period |
| $u(n, \tau)$ | binary | – | 1 when charging, 0 when discharging |
| $S_{\max}(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MWh | effective storage volume after degradation |
| $n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | cycles | equivalent full cycles accumulated along $\text{path}(n)$ |

$u$ is a **recourse** integer variable: one binary per node and period, chosen
after that node's prices are known. §11 records what that costs.

Two conventions carry the state across a boundary. For every $n$ and every
state variable $v \in \{S, n_{\text{cyc}}\}$,

$$
v(n, 0) \;:=\;
\begin{cases}
v\bigl(a(n), W\bigr) & n \neq r \\[4pt]
0 & n = r
\end{cases}
$$

so that $S(r, 0) = 0$ and $n_{\text{cyc}}(r, 0) = 0$ reproduce the initial
conditions of M§4.2 and M§4.6.

## 4. Objective

Maximise expected profit. Period revenue is M§3 evaluated at the node's prices,

$$
R(n, \tau) \;=\; \Delta t \Bigl[\, p_1(n,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_1(n,\tau) - c_1(n,\tau)\bigr) \;+\; p_2(n,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_2(n,\tau) - c_2(n,\tau)\bigr) \Bigr]
$$

and the expectation over the tree weights each node by its probability:

$$
\max \;\; \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} R(t)\Bigr] \;=\; \max \;\; \sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}} \pi(n) \sum_{\tau = 1}^{W} R(n, \tau)
$$

The equality is the tower property applied to the tree, and it is what makes the
expectation a single linear objective over all node copies rather than a
nested one. Profit along a single scenario is written

$$
Z(\omega) \;=\; \sum_{n \in \text{path}(\omega)} \; \sum_{\tau=1}^{W} R(n, \tau) \qquad \omega \in \mathcal{L}
$$

and is used by §7 and §9.

## 5. Constraints

Each of the following holds **for every node $n \in \mathcal{N}$ and every
$\tau = 1, \dots, W$** unless stated otherwise. Where a constraint is M§4
unchanged, that is said rather than re-argued.

### 5.1 Storage bounds

M§4.1, per node.

$$
0 \;\leq\; S(n, \tau) \;\leq\; S_{\max}(n, \tau)
$$

### 5.2 Energy balance within a node

M§4.2, per node, with the within-node recursion running over $\tau$.

$$
S(n, \tau) \;=\; S(n, \tau - 1) + \Delta t \,\Bigl[(1 - e_c)\bigl(c_1(n,\tau) + c_2(n,\tau)\bigr) - d_1(n,\tau) - d_2(n,\tau)\Bigr]
$$

### 5.3 State handover at a node boundary

The convention of §3 states this as a definition rather than a row, and it is
the only coupling between stages:

$$
S(n, 0) = S\bigl(a(n), W\bigr), \qquad n_{\text{cyc}}(n, 0) = n_{\text{cyc}}\bigl(a(n), W\bigr) \qquad \forall n \neq r
$$

Every child of a node opens from the **same** physical state, because the state
is the consequence of decisions already taken at the parent. The scenarios
diverge in their prices, not in where the battery starts.

### 5.4 Complementarity and rate limits

M§4.3, per node, with its own binary.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 \;\leq\; c_1(n,\tau) + c_2(n,\tau) &\;\leq\; \overline{C} \, u(n,\tau) \\[4pt]
0 \;\leq\; d_1(n,\tau) + d_2(n,\tau) &\;\leq\; \overline{D} \, \bigl(1 - u(n,\tau)\bigr)
\end{aligned}
$$

### 5.5 Market 2 hourly commitment

M§4.5, within a node. Writing $\mathcal{T}_h(n)$ for the two within-node periods
of hour $h$,

$$
c_2(n, \tau) = c_2(n, \tau'), \qquad d_2(n, \tau) = d_2(n, \tau') \qquad \forall\, \tau, \tau' \in \mathcal{T}_h(n),\ \forall h
$$

$W$ being a whole number of hours means no hour is split across stages, so this
is the whole of M§4.5.

### 5.6 Commitment measurability

The non-anticipativity of §2.1, made explicit for a market committed $\ell_m$
stages before delivery. For $m \in \{1, 2\}$ with $\ell_m \geq 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_m(n, \tau) &= c_m(n', \tau) \\[2pt]
d_m(n, \tau) &= d_m(n', \tau)
\end{aligned}
\qquad \forall\, n, n' \in \mathcal{N}_k \ \text{ with } \ \mathrm{anc}^{\ell_m}(n) = \mathrm{anc}^{\ell_m}(n'), \quad \forall k \geq \ell_m
$$

For $\ell_m = 0$ the constraint is vacuous, non-anticipativity being already
implied by indexing the variable at the node. Note that the volume is shared
across siblings while the revenue in §4 is evaluated at each sibling's own
price: a commitment made before the price is known earns what that node turns
out to pay.

The binary $u$ and the Market 1 volumes are left free per node under the default
$\ell_1 = 0$, so the battery may always re-decide *within* the hour of a Market
2 commitment it has already made, subject to §5.4 and §5.5.

### 5.7 Cycle accounting

M§4.6's accumulation, per node, on the discharge side only.

$$
n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau) \;=\; n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau - 1) + \frac{\Delta t \,\bigl(d_1(n,\tau) + d_2(n,\tau)\bigr)}{\overline{S}_{\max}}
$$

The count is path dependent: $n_{\text{cyc}}(n, W)$ is the throughput along
$\text{path}(n)$ and nothing else, which is why the cap below is a statement
about paths.

### 5.8 Cycle life under uncertainty

M§4.6 caps one number because there is one future. Here there are many, and the
cap admits three readings. The default is the first.

**(a) Almost surely.** The budget holds on every realisation:

$$
n_{\text{cyc}}(\omega, W) \;\leq\; \overline{L} \qquad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L}
$$

One row per scenario. Cycle life is a physical property of the asset, not an
average over futures that did not happen, so this is the reading that matches
the brief. As in M§4.6, $n_{\text{cyc}}$ is non-decreasing along a path, so
capping the leaf caps every node above it, and the per-node form

$$
n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau) \leq \overline{L} \quad \forall n, \tau
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
n_{\text{cyc}}(\omega, W) \leq \overline{L} \quad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L}
$$

defines the same feasible set.

**(b) In expectation.** The budget binds on average:

$$
\sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \; n_{\text{cyc}}(\omega, W) \;\leq\; \overline{L}
$$

One row for the whole tree, and a weaker constraint than (a): it permits
scenarios that exceed the cycle life, offset by scenarios that undershoot. It
reads cycle life as a budget of value rather than a physical limit, which may
be right if the asset can be refurbished at a known price, and is otherwise an
accounting convenience.

**(c) With a chance constraint.** The budget may be exceeded with probability at
most $\varepsilon$. With a binary $y(\omega) \in \{0,1\}$ per scenario and
$\overline{Z}$ large enough to be non-binding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{\text{cyc}}(\omega, W) &\;\leq\; \overline{L} + \overline{Z} \, y(\omega) \qquad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L} \\[4pt]
\sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \, y(\omega) &\;\leq\; \varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

This adds $|\mathcal{L}|$ binaries and a big-$M$ whose looseness weakens the
relaxation, so it is the most expensive of the three. $\varepsilon = 0$
recovers (a).

Under any of the three, a $\lambda$ priced against the cap is the shadow price
of that cap, with the same interpretation as M§6 and a different domain: in (a)
there is one multiplier per scenario, in (b) exactly one for the tree.

### 5.9 Storage volume degradation

M§4.7, per node, linear in the node's accumulated count.

$$
S_{\max}(n, \tau) \;=\; \overline{S}_{\max}\,\bigl(1 - \rho \, n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau)\bigr)
$$

As in M§4.7 the variable may be substituted out of §5.1, leaving one row per
node and period in $S$ and $n_{\text{cyc}}$. Because $n_{\text{cyc}}$ is path
dependent, so is the usable volume: two scenarios reaching the same stage hold
batteries of different size, which is a real effect of the policy and not an
artefact of the tree.

### 5.10 Terminal condition

M§4.2 fixes $S(0) = 0$ and leaves $S(T)$ free, which is defensible over three
years of a ten-year life. On a tree the choice matters more, because a policy
that empties the store at every leaf is rewarded for behaviour that has no
consequence inside the horizon. Three options, in increasing order of what they
assume:

$$
\text{(i) } S(\omega, W) \ \text{free}
\qquad
\text{(ii) } S(\omega, W) = S(r, 0)
\qquad
\text{(iii) } \max \ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\textstyle\sum R\bigr] + \sum_{\omega} \pi(\omega) \, V\bigl(S(\omega, W),\, n_{\text{cyc}}(\omega, W)\bigr)
$$

(i) is M§4.2 unchanged. (ii) is the cyclic condition of M§5, which removes the
end-of-horizon windfall without pricing it. (iii) attaches a terminal value
function $V$, concave and piecewise linear in its arguments so the program stays
a MILP, and is the right choice when the horizon is a truncation of a longer
life rather than the life itself. $V \equiv 0$ recovers (i).

## 6. The deterministic equivalent, and its size

§4 and §5 together are one mixed-integer linear program over the variables of
all nodes at once, the **deterministic equivalent**. It is a MILP for the reason
given in §2.3: prices enter only as node data, so each node's revenue is linear
in that node's decisions, and every constraint of §5 is linear.

Its size is the tree's. With uniform branching $b$ at each stage,

$$
|\mathcal{N}| \;=\; \frac{b^{K} - 1}{b - 1},
\qquad
|\mathcal{L}| \;=\; b^{K-1},
\qquad
\text{binaries} \;=\; W \, |\mathcal{N}|
$$

so the program grows geometrically in the number of stages. Three illustrations,
all with a daily stage, $W = 48$:

| Stages $K$ | Branching $b$ | Nodes | Binaries | Period-instances |
| --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| 7 (one week) | 2 | 127 | 6,096 | 6,096 |
| 7 (one week) | 3 | 1,093 | 52,464 | 52,464 |
| 14 (fortnight) | 3 | 2,391,484 | 114,791,232 | 114,791,232 |
| 1,096 (three years) | 2 | $2^{1096} - 1$ | — | — |

The second row is the useful comparison. A **one-week** tree that branches three
ways a day carries 52,464 binaries, which is the size of the **three-year**
deterministic program of M§6, and that program could not be proved optimal in an
hour ([`tractability.md`](tractability.md)). The last row is written only to make
the growth explicit: a tree over three years of daily stages has more nodes than
can be enumerated, whatever the hardware.

Two consequences for how this formulation can be used, both structural rather
than computational:

- The deterministic equivalent is a statement of the problem, not a plan for
  solving it. It is worth writing down because it fixes exactly what a solution
  method must approximate.
- The horizon and the tree are separate approximations. A policy may be computed
  on a short tree and applied in a rolling fashion over the long horizon, in
  which case the tree represents the uncertainty that matters for the decision
  being committed now, and the state handover of §5.3 carries the rest.

## 7. Risk aversion: mean-CVaR

Expected profit is indifferent to the spread of outcomes. A mean-risk objective
replaces §4 with a weighted combination of the expectation and the conditional
value at risk of the lower tail of profit. Using the Rockafellar and Uryasev
representation, which is linear once an auxiliary variable per scenario is
added:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max \quad & (1 - \beta) \sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \, Z(\omega) \;+\; \beta \Bigl[ \eta - \frac{1}{\alpha} \sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \, z(\omega) \Bigr] \\[4pt]
\text{s.t.} \quad & z(\omega) \;\geq\; \eta - Z(\omega) \qquad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L} \\[4pt]
& z(\omega) \;\geq\; 0 \qquad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L} \\[4pt]
& \eta \ \text{free}
\end{aligned}
$$

with $Z(\omega)$ from §4. At the optimum $\eta$ is the $\alpha$-quantile of
profit and the bracket is $\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha$, the mean of the worst $\alpha$
fraction of outcomes. $\beta = 0$ recovers §4 exactly; $\beta = 1$ maximises the
tail alone.

The cost is $|\mathcal{L}| + 1$ continuous variables and $|\mathcal{L}|$ rows,
with no new integers, so the program remains a MILP of the same order of
difficulty. The constraints of §5 are untouched: risk aversion changes what is
preferred, not what is possible.

## 8. The scenario form

An equivalent statement indexes variables by scenario rather than by node, and
imposes non-anticipativity explicitly. Let every $\omega \in \mathcal{L}$ carry
its own full-horizon copy $x(\omega)$ of the variables of M§2, subject to all of
M§4 along that scenario's prices. The program is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max \quad & \sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \, Z\bigl(x(\omega)\bigr) \\[4pt]
\text{s.t.} \quad & x(\omega) \in \mathcal{X}(\omega) \qquad \forall \omega \in \mathcal{L} \\[4pt]
& x_k(\omega) = x_k(\omega') \qquad \forall k, \ \forall \omega, \omega' \ \text{sharing the stage-}k\text{ node}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathcal{X}(\omega)$ is the deterministic feasible set of M§4 under
scenario $\omega$'s prices, and the last line is the **split-variable** or
non-anticipativity constraint.

The two forms have the same optimum. The node form (§4 to §5) is smaller, having
one copy per node rather than one per scenario per stage, and is what a direct
solve would use. The scenario form is larger but separable once the coupling row
is relaxed, each scenario then being exactly the program of `model.md`, which is
the structure progressive hedging and scenario decomposition exploit. Writing
both down costs a page and records that the choice between them is a solution
decision rather than a modelling one.

## 9. Special cases and checks

The formulation should be read with these reductions in mind. Each is a test a
correct implementation would have to pass.

**It collapses to the deterministic model.** With $b = 1$ at every stage the
tree is a single path, $|\mathcal{L}| = 1$, §5.6 is vacuous, §5.8(a) is one row,
and §4 to §5 become M§3 to M§4.7 exactly, with the same optimum. Perfect
foresight is the degenerate case of this model, not a different one.

**It is bounded by perfect foresight.** Solving `model.md` separately on each
scenario and averaging gives the wait-and-see value

$$
\mathrm{WS} \;=\; \sum_{\omega \in \mathcal{L}} \pi(\omega) \; V_{\det}(\omega),
\qquad
\mathrm{EVPI} \;=\; \mathrm{WS} - \mathrm{RP} \;\geq\; 0
$$

where $\mathrm{RP}$ is the optimum of §4 to §5 and $V_{\det}(\omega)$ is the
deterministic optimum under $\omega$. EVPI, the expected value of perfect
information, is what foresight is worth and therefore the ceiling on what any
better forecast could add. The inequality holds because a single policy cannot
beat a different schedule chosen per scenario.

**It is bounded below by the deterministic solution it replaces.** Solve
`model.md` once on the mean price path, fix the decisions §5.6 requires to be
committed, evaluate the expectation of the rest, and call the result
$\mathrm{EEV}$. Then

$$
\mathrm{VSS} \;=\; \mathrm{RP} - \mathrm{EEV} \;\geq\; 0
$$

the value of the stochastic solution: what is gained by modelling the
uncertainty rather than its average. VSS is the number that decides whether this
formulation is worth solving at all, and it is zero whenever the deterministic
policy happens to be optimal under uncertainty.

**The ordering of the three cycle-life readings.** Since (a) implies (b) and
$\varepsilon = 0$ in (c) is (a),

$$
\mathrm{RP}_{(a)} \;\leq\; \mathrm{RP}_{(c)} \;\leq\; \mathrm{RP}_{(b)}
$$

so reading the cap in expectation never reports less profit than reading it
almost surely. A comparison between studies has to state which reading it used.

## 10. Assumptions this formulation adds

Beyond those of M§5, which all carry over:

- **The price process is exogenous and the battery remains a price taker.** The
  tree is fixed before optimisation; no decision changes a probability or a
  price. Without this the objective would be bilinear and the program would stop
  being a MILP.
- **A finite tree represents the filtration.** The continuous price process is
  replaced by finitely many realisations with known probabilities. The tree is
  an input here, and its construction, whether by sampling, by moment matching
  or by a lattice, is not part of this formulation. Any quantity computed from
  it is conditional on it.
- **Stage and settlement frequency are separate choices.** $W$ fixes how often
  the policy may re-plan against new information; it is not a property of the
  markets. A daily stage asserts that a day's prices become known as a block.
- **The state is the pair $(S, n_{\text{cyc}})$ and nothing else.** §5.3 carries
  exactly these across a boundary, as in M§6. If degradation acquired a calendar
  component, or the cycle count could recover with rest, the state would widen
  and §5.3 with it.
- **Commitment lags are integral multiples of a stage.** §5.6 is written in
  stages, so a gate closure that falls inside a stage cannot be represented
  without splitting the stage.

## 11. What is deferred

This document states the model. Three questions it does not answer, recorded so
that the omission is visible:

- **How the tree is built.** Scenario generation, reduction and the testing of
  whether a tree represents the price process are prior to this formulation and
  independent of it.
- **How it is solved.** §6 shows the deterministic equivalent is beyond direct
  solution at any interesting size. Candidate methods, including scenario
  decomposition on the form of §8 and dynamic programming over the state of
  §5.3, differ in what they assume about the tree. One obstacle belongs here
  rather than there, because it is a property of the model: the recourse
  variables $u(n, \tau)$ of §5.4 are **integer**, so the value function over the
  state of §5.3 is not convex, and methods that approximate it with cutting
  planes are not valid without further work.
- **What it is worth.** VSS and EVPI in §9 are definitions, not measurements.
  On this data the deterministic model already reaches within 0.41% of its own
  optimum ([`tractability.md`](tractability.md)) and one day of foresight
  captures nearly all of the value available to it
  ([`model.md`](model.md) §6), so the quantity to establish first is whether
  VSS is large enough to justify any of the above.
