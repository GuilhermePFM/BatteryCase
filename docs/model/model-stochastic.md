# Battery dispatch under stochastic prices

Formulation of the battery dispatch problem when the two price series are not
known in advance, under a day-ahead commitment structure: a multistage
stochastic mixed-integer linear program whose deterministic counterpart is
[`model.md`](model.md).

Throughout, **M§4.2** means §4.2 of that document. This file states the model
only. Nothing here is implemented, and no solution method is proposed; §10 says
what is deferred and why.

The formulation rests on five modelling choices, stated once here and used
everywhere below:

| Choice | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| a stage is a day, $W = 48$ | prices are re-forecast daily; the tree branches once a day |
| both markets are committed the day before | every volume for day $k$ is chosen at the end of day $k-1$ |
| the objective is expected profit | risk neutral, no tail term |
| the cycle budget holds almost surely | it is a physical limit, not an average |
| the terminal stock is free | as in M§4.2 |

Together they buy a considerable simplification, set out in §4.2: the program
reduces to a **deterministic** MILP on the tree, in which each node carries the
conditionally expected prices of the following day. §4.3 says exactly which of
the five choices that reduction depends on.

---

## 1. What changes, and what does not

M§1 takes both price series as exogenous **and known**: the whole horizon is
given before any decision is made, so the plan is a single sequence chosen
with perfect foresight. The battery remains a price taker here, and the series
remain exogenous, but they are no longer known. *A volume delivered on day $k$
must be chosen using only what has been observed up to the end of day $k-1$.*

Two things follow:

- The decision is no longer a plan but a **policy**: a rule mapping
  observed history to a day's plan. On a finite scenario tree a policy is
  represented by one copy of the decision variables per node.
- The objective is a **functional of a distribution**, here the expectation.

The physics does not change. Every constraint of M§4.1 through M§4.7 holds in
each node exactly as written, because none of them involves a price.

## 2. Uncertainty and information

### 2.1 Probability space and filtration

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ carry the joint price process
$\bigl(p_1(t), p_2(t)\bigr)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$, and let

$$
\mathcal{F}_{-1} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{K-1} = \mathcal{F}
$$

be the filtration generated by observing prices day by day, with
$\mathcal{F}_{k}$ the information available once day $k$ has settled and
$\mathcal{F}_{-1}$ trivial. The day-ahead commitment of §2.3 is the statement
that the plan for day $k$ is $\mathcal{F}_{k-1}$-measurable. That requirement is
**non-anticipativity**, and it is the only structural addition to M§4.

### 2.2 The scenario tree

The filtration is represented by a finite tree, one level per day, with a root
that carries no prices because nothing has been observed before day 0.

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $k = 0, \dots, K-1$ | day |
| $\mathcal{N}_k$ | the nodes carrying day $k$'s prices; $\mathcal{N} = \{r\} \cup \bigcup_k \mathcal{N}_k$ |
| $r$ | the root, the state before day 0 |
| $a(n)$ | the parent of node $n \neq r$ |
| $\mathcal{C}(n)$ | the children of $n$ |
| $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{N}_{K-1}$ | the leaves, one per scenario |
| $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{N} \setminus \mathcal{L}$ | the **decision nodes**: those with children |
| $\pi(n) > 0$ | the probability of reaching $n$ |

with $\pi(r) = 1$ and $\pi(n) = \sum_{n' \in \mathcal{C}(n)} \pi(n')$, hence
$\sum_{n \in \mathcal{N}_k} \pi(n) = 1$ for every day $k$. A node is an
information state: two scenarios sharing a node are indistinguishable up to
that day.

Each node $n \neq r$ carries the realised price vectors $p_1(n, \tau)$ and
$p_2(n, \tau)$ for the $\tau = 1, \dots, 48$ settlement periods of its day.
These are **data of the node**, not variables, which is what keeps the program
linear. Because a node holds the joint realisation of both markets, any
dependence between $p_1$ and $p_2$, contemporaneous or lagged, is represented
exactly by the tree and needs no separate correlation structure.

### 2.3 Who decides what, and when

Both markets have gate closure on the previous day. The plan for a day is
therefore chosen at that day's **parent**, and is necessarily the same for all
children of that parent, since they are indistinguishable at the moment of
commitment. Attaching the decision to the node where it is taken rather than to
the node where it is delivered makes non-anticipativity automatic and removes
the need for any explicit measurability row:

> For each $n \in \mathcal{D}$, the variables of §3 indexed by $n$ are the plan
> for the day following $n$, committed knowing $n$'s history, and delivered
> under each child's prices.

```
      r              day 0            day 1            day 2
 commit day 0 ─┬──→ [prices]  ─┬──→  [prices]  ─┬──→  [prices]   ω₁
               │   commit d.1  │    commit d.2  └──→  [prices]   ω₂
               │               └──→  [prices]  ─┬──→  [prices]   ω₃
               │                    commit d.2  └──→  [prices]   ω₄
               └──→ [prices]  ──→      ...
                   commit d.1

    decisions and state live on 𝒟;  prices live on the children
```

Leaves carry prices but no decisions: their day was committed at their parent.
The root carries decisions but no prices: day 0 is committed under the prior.

## 3. Notation

Sets and parameters of M§2 carry over unchanged, with $\Delta t = \tfrac12$ h
and $W = 48$ periods in a day. Added:

| Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $K$ | days | horizon in days; $T = 48K$ |
| $\pi(n)$ | – | probability of node $n$ |
| $\bar p_m(n, \tau)$ | £/MWh | conditionally expected price, §4.2 |

Every decision variable of M§2 gains a decision-node index and a within-day
period index. For $n \in \mathcal{D}$ and $\tau = 1, \dots, 48$:

| Symbol | Domain | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $c_1(n, \tau),\ c_2(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MW | charging power bought from each market |
| $d_1(n, \tau),\ d_2(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MW | discharging power drawn from storage for each market |
| $S(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MWh | energy stored at the end of the period |
| $u(n, \tau)$ | binary | – | 1 when charging, 0 when discharging |
| $S_{\max}(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | MWh | effective storage volume after degradation |
| $n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau)$ | $\geq 0$ | cycles | equivalent full cycles accumulated along $\text{path}(n)$ |

$u$ is **not** a recourse variable here: like every other volume it is fixed the
day before, so the day's charge/discharge pattern is committed in full.

Two conventions carry the state across a day boundary. For every
$n \in \mathcal{D}$ and every state variable
$v \in \{S, n_{\text{cyc}}\}$,

$$
v(n, 0) \;:=\;
\begin{cases}
v\bigl(a(n), 48\bigr) & n \neq r \\[4pt]
0 & n = r
\end{cases}
$$

so that $S(r, 0) = 0$ and $n_{\text{cyc}}(r, 0) = 0$ reproduce the initial
conditions of M§4.2 and M§4.6.

## 4. Objective

### 4.1 Expected profit

Revenue is M§3, evaluated at the prices the delivering node turns out to pay.
For $n \in \mathcal{D}$ and a child $m \in \mathcal{C}(n)$,

$$
R(n, m, \tau) \;=\; \Delta t \Bigl[\, p_1(m,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_1(n,\tau) - c_1(n,\tau)\bigr) \;+\; p_2(m,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_2(n,\tau) - c_2(n,\tau)\bigr) \Bigr]
$$

and the expectation over the tree weights each delivery by its probability:

$$
\max \;\; \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} R(t)\Bigr] \;=\; \max \;\; \sum_{n \in \mathcal{D}} \; \sum_{m \in \mathcal{C}(n)} \pi(m) \sum_{\tau = 1}^{48} R(n, m, \tau)
$$

The equality is the tower property applied to the tree: it is what makes the
expectation a single linear objective over all node copies rather than a nested
one.

### 4.2 The objective collapses onto conditional mean prices

Within the inner sum only the prices depend on $m$; the volumes are the same
for every child, because they were committed before the child was known. Define
the **conditionally expected price** of the day following $n$,

$$
\bar p_m(n, \tau) \;=\; \sum_{m' \in \mathcal{C}(n)} \frac{\pi(m')}{\pi(n)} \; p_m(m', \tau), \qquad m \in \{1, 2\}
$$

Substituting and collecting terms gives the objective in its working form:

$$
\max \;\; \sum_{n \in \mathcal{D}} \pi(n) \sum_{\tau=1}^{48} \Delta t \Bigl[\, \bar p_1(n,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_1(n,\tau) - c_1(n,\tau)\bigr) \;+\; \bar p_2(n,\tau)\bigl((1 - e_d)\,d_2(n,\tau) - c_2(n,\tau)\bigr) \Bigr]
$$

No price uncertainty remains anywhere in the program. Each decision node sees a
single price vector, its conditional mean, and the realisations enter only
through that mean. This is **certainty equivalence**, and it holds for three
reasons together: the decisions are deterministic given the node, revenue is
linear in the prices, and no constraint contains a price. It is the reason the
rest of this document is short.

What survives of the uncertainty is the tree itself. Two nodes at the same day
with different histories hold different conditional means and therefore plan
differently, which is precisely what makes the solution a policy rather than a
plan.

## 5. Constraints

Each of the following holds **for every decision node $n \in \mathcal{D}$ and
every $\tau = 1, \dots, 48$** unless stated otherwise. Where a constraint is M§4
unchanged, that is said rather than re-argued.

### 5.1 Storage bounds

M§4.1, per node.

$$
0 \;\leq\; S(n, \tau) \;\leq\; S_{\max}(n, \tau)
$$

### 5.2 Energy balance within a day

M§4.2, per node, with the recursion running over the day's periods.

$$
S(n, \tau) \;=\; S(n, \tau - 1) + \Delta t \,\Bigl[(1 - e_c)\bigl(c_1(n,\tau) + c_2(n,\tau)\bigr) - d_1(n,\tau) - d_2(n,\tau)\Bigr]
$$

### 5.3 State handover at a day boundary

The convention of §3 states this as a definition rather than a row, and it is
the only coupling between days:

$$
S(n, 0) = S\bigl(a(n), 48\bigr), \qquad n_{\text{cyc}}(n, 0) = n_{\text{cyc}}\bigl(a(n), 48\bigr) \qquad \forall n \in \mathcal{D},\ n \neq r
$$

Every child opens from the **same** physical state, because the plan that
produced it was committed before the children were distinguishable. The
scenarios diverge in their prices, not in where the battery starts, and not in
what it did yesterday.

### 5.4 Complementarity and rate limits

M§4.3 and M§4.4, per node, with its own binary.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 \;\leq\; c_1(n,\tau) + c_2(n,\tau) &\;\leq\; \overline{C} \, u(n,\tau) \\[4pt]
0 \;\leq\; d_1(n,\tau) + d_2(n,\tau) &\;\leq\; \overline{D} \, \bigl(1 - u(n,\tau)\bigr)
\end{aligned}
$$

### 5.5 Market 2 hourly commitment

M§4.5, within the day. Writing $\mathcal{T}_h$ for the two periods of hour $h$,

$$
c_2(n, \tau) = c_2(n, \tau'), \qquad d_2(n, \tau) = d_2(n, \tau') \qquad \forall\, \tau, \tau' \in \mathcal{T}_h,\ \forall h = 1, \dots, 24
$$

A day is a whole number of hours, so no hour is split across nodes and this is
the whole of M§4.5. Note that it is a statement about *granularity* and remains
necessary: day-ahead commitment fixes when the volume is chosen, not that
Market 2 is sold in hourly blocks while Market 1 is sold by the half hour.

### 5.6 Cycle accounting and cycle life

M§4.6's accumulation, per node, on the discharge side only.

$$
n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau) \;=\; n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau - 1) + \frac{\Delta t \,\bigl(d_1(n,\tau) + d_2(n,\tau)\bigr)}{\overline{S}_{\max}}
$$

The count is path dependent: $n_{\text{cyc}}(n, 48)$ is the throughput along the
path from $r$ to $n$ and nothing else. The budget holds on every realisation,

$$
n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau) \;\leq\; \overline{L} \qquad \forall n \in \mathcal{D},\ \forall \tau
$$

Cycle life is a physical property of the asset, not an average over futures that
did not happen. As in M§4.6, $n_{\text{cyc}}$ is non-decreasing along a path, so
capping each node caps every scenario: because every scenario's total is
attained at the last decision node on its path, the per-node form above and the
per-scenario form $n_{\text{cyc}}(a(\omega), 48) \leq \overline{L}$ for all
$\omega \in \mathcal{L}$ define the same feasible set.

A $\lambda$ calibrated against this cap is its shadow price, with the same
interpretation as M§6: one multiplier per node, each the marginal value of a
cycle on that history.

### 5.7 Storage volume degradation

M§4.7, per node, linear in the node's accumulated count.

$$
S_{\max}(n, \tau) \;=\; \overline{S}_{\max}\,\bigl(1 - \rho \, n_{\text{cyc}}(n, \tau)\bigr)
$$

As in M§4.7 the variable may be substituted out of §5.1, leaving one row per
node and period in $S$ and $n_{\text{cyc}}$. Because $n_{\text{cyc}}$ is path
dependent, so is the usable volume: two nodes of the same day hold batteries of
different size, which is a real effect of the policy and not an artefact of the
tree.

### 5.8 Terminal condition

$S(n, 48)$ is left free at the last decision nodes, as M§4.2 leaves $S(T)$ free.
This is defensible over a horizon that is short beside the asset's life, and it
is what keeps §4.2's collapse intact; the cost is that the policy is rewarded
for emptying the store at the end of the horizon, so a long rolling study should
either impose $S(n,48) = S(r,0)$ or attach a terminal value, and accept the
consequences listed in §4.3.

## 6. The deterministic equivalent, and its size

§4 and §5 together are one mixed-integer linear program over the variables of
all decision nodes at once. By §4.2 it is a MILP with *no* stochastic element
left in its rows: the tree supplies the branching and the conditional means, and
everything else reads exactly like M§4.

Its size is the tree's. With uniform branching $b$ at each day,

$$
|\mathcal{N}| \;=\; \frac{b^{K+1} - 1}{b - 1},
\qquad
|\mathcal{L}| \;=\; b^{K},
\qquad
|\mathcal{D}| \;=\; \frac{b^{K} - 1}{b - 1},
\qquad
\text{binaries} \;=\; 48 \, |\mathcal{D}|
$$

so the program grows geometrically in the horizon:

| Days $K$ | Branching $b$ | Decision nodes | Binaries |
| --: | --: | --: | --: |
| 7 (one week) | 2 | 127 | 6,096 |
| 7 (one week) | 3 | 1,093 | 52,464 |
| 14 (fortnight) | 3 | 2,391,484 | 114,791,232 |
| 1,096 (three years) | 2 | $2^{1096} - 1$ | — |

The second row is the useful comparison in one respect only: a **one-week** tree
that branches three ways a day carries as many binaries as the **three-year**
deterministic program of M§6. It should not be read as implying the same
difficulty. That program's hour was spent entirely at the root
([`tractability.md`](tractability.md): `Nodes 0`, the search never branched), on a
relaxation weakened by a cycle budget coupling 1,096 days; over a week that budget
does not bind and the paths are short. The point of the table is the exponent, not
the row: a tree over three years of daily stages has more nodes than can be
enumerated, whatever the hardware and whatever the decomposition, which is why a
policy must be computed on a short tree and rolled.

Two consequences, both structural rather than computational:

- The deterministic equivalent states the problem; it is not the form in which
  the problem would be handed to a solver. 
- The horizon and the tree are separate approximations. A policy may be computed
  on a short tree and applied in a rolling fashion over the long horizon, in
  which case the tree represents the uncertainty that matters for the commitment
  being made tonight, and the handover of §5.3 carries the rest. Under that
  scheme the tree built tonight spans the few days over which the conditional
  mean still moves with what was observed today, not the study horizon, and is
  correspondingly small.

### 6.1 Solving method: decomposition

The program is already structured for decomposition, and in the same way the
deterministic model is: days couple only through the two-dimensional state
$(S, n_{\text{cyc}})$ of §5.3. Relax that handover and what is left is one
48-period MILP per decision node, each the size of a single day and each solved in
milliseconds. Two families exploit this, and they split the program along
different seams:

- **By node, along the state.** Treating $(S, n_{\text{cyc}})$ as the state of a
  dynamic program is what makes the rolling decomposition of
  [`tractability.md`](tractability.md) work on the deterministic model, and the
  tree is its stochastic analogue: a value function per day, estimated from the
  children. Stochastic dual dynamic programming is the standard method here, and
  it is **not valid as it stands**, because the $u(n, \tau)$ of §5.4 are integer
  and the value function over the state is therefore not convex. Making it valid means Lagrangian cuts over a binarised state, as in
  SDDiP, or accepting an approximation whose error is not bounded by the method.
- **By scenario, along the commitment.** Relaxing the split-variable rows of §9
  leaves one copy of `model.md` per scenario, each solvable by the means already
  in use. Progressive hedging and dual decomposition both work this way. Neither
  terminates with a certificate on a mixed-integer program: they return a primal
  plan and a dual bound, and the gap between them is the answer's quality
  rather than a proof of optimality.
